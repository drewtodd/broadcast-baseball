

# Commentary Style Guide — Broadcast Baseball

This document defines **how commentary should sound** in Broadcast Baseball.
It translates the high-level “radio-first” philosophy into concrete, enforceable rules.

If a line of commentary violates these rules, it should be changed or removed,
even if it is factually correct.

---

## Core Principles

### Speak Like a Broadcaster, Not a Narrator

Commentary should sound like it is being spoken live.

- Write for the ear, not the eye
- Prefer natural phrasing over clever wording
- Avoid literary flourish unless the moment truly earns it

If it sounds strange when read aloud, it does not belong.

---

### One Idea per Line

Each line of commentary should communicate **one primary idea**.

Avoid:
- Multiple clauses joined by commas
- Explaining cause and effect in a single breath
- Packing setup and payoff into the same line

Prefer:
- Short, focused sentences
- Letting silence do some of the work

---

## Commentary Roles

### Play-by-Play (PBP)

Play-by-play is the backbone of the broadcast.

**Rules:**
- Always factual
- Deterministic and reproducible
- Concise
- Never speculative

**Responsibilities:**
- Describe what happened
- Establish or reinforce the count when relevant
- Clearly signal terminal outcomes (walk, strikeout, ball in play)

**Prohibitions:**
- No predictions
- No internal simulation details
- No emotional interpretation beyond neutral tone

Example:
- “Fastball low. Ball one.”
- “Swing and a miss. Strike two.”
- “Ground ball to short. Throw to first… in time.”

---

### Color Commentary

Color commentary is optional and additive.

**Rules:**
- Must not contradict play-by-play
- Must not introduce new facts
- Must respect pacing and silence
- Should be brief

**Purpose:**
- Add context
- Highlight tension
- Reinforce narrative threads already established

**Prohibitions:**
- No outcome control
- No invented statistics
- No analysis that requires visuals to understand

Example:
- “He’s fallen behind a few hitters tonight.”
- “This is where the count really matters.”

---

## Tone and Restraint

### Routine Moments Stay Routine

Most pitches are routine.
Commentary should reflect that.

Avoid:
- Overhyping early-count pitches
- Repeating similar phrasing back-to-back
- Excessive emotion without cause

Big moments are only big if the small ones stay small.

---

### Big Moments Earn Space

When the moment is significant:
- Lines may be slightly longer
- Pauses may be more frequent
- Color commentary may appear more often

Even then, restraint matters.
A full count is tense; it is not a walk-off.

---

## Count Awareness

The count shapes everything.

Guidelines:
- Reference the count explicitly in leverage situations
- Let tone change naturally with the count
- Use longer pauses as counts deepen

Long at-bats should feel like a progression, not a reset on every pitch.

---

## Language Guidelines

### Prefer Familiar Baseball Language

Use phrasing that would sound natural on a radio broadcast.

Prefer:
- “Swing and a miss”
- “Fouled straight back”
- “Takes low”
- “Grounder to short”

Avoid:
- Technical jargon
- Overly modern analytics terms (unless intentionally added later)
- Visual shorthand without context

---

### Names and Identification

- Use player names sparingly
- Repeating a name is acceptable if it aids clarity
- Avoid full stat lines in commentary

Listeners should never be confused about who is involved in the play.

---

## Pauses and Cadence

Pauses are part of the style.

Internal conventions may be used to represent pauses (e.g., ellipses or markers),
but the guiding rule is simple:

> Pauses should feel natural when spoken aloud.

Overusing pauses is as harmful as never using them.

---

## Anti-Patterns

Avoid the following:

- Commentary that explains the rules of baseball
- Commentary that narrates the UI or visuals
- Commentary that feels like a recap instead of a live call
- Commentary that attempts to be clever every pitch

---

## Enforcement Rule

When deciding between:
- More detail  
- Clearer pacing  

Always choose **clearer pacing**.

---

## Guiding Question

For any line of commentary, ask:

> **Would this sound right coming through a car radio at night?**

If the answer is no, revise it.
